Assess your firm for the business-ending event.

If your security meetings are spent arguing over DLP products, you are probably missing the one scenario that matters.

Cablons Cyber. November 2026. About eight minutes.

Sit in enough security steering meetings and you notice a pattern. The agenda is a list of products. Which data loss prevention tool to buy. Whether the email filter should be swapped for the one the reseller is pushing this quarter. Whether the endpoint agent needs upgrading to the tier with the dashboard. Each of these is a reasonable question, and each will eat an hour. What rarely appears on the agenda is the question that should have come first: what is the event that ends this company, and how well are we placed to survive it?

That is not a rhetorical question. For most mid-sized firms there are one or two scenarios that would be fatal within weeks, and a long tail of incidents that would be expensive, embarrassing and survivable. A security programme that treats these as the same thing, as a list of controls to be bought and ticked, is spending its budget in proportion to vendor marketing rather than in proportion to risk.

The controls-first trap

The DLP argument is a good example because it feels like serious security work. Data leaving the business is a real risk, the products are complex, and the evaluation generates a satisfying amount of activity. But ask what happens to the company if a spreadsheet of customer names leaks. In most cases the answer is an uncomfortable conversation with the ICO, some customer churn, a bruised reputation and a bad quarter. Painful, but the firm is still trading a year later.

Now ask what happens if the firm is ransomwared on a Thursday night and the attackers have had domain admin for a fortnight. Not the headline version where files are encrypted and a note appears. The operational version. The warehouse management system is gone and the backups were on the same domain. Nobody can pick, pack or raise a delivery note. The building management system that runs the cold store was reachable from the corporate network, and when it failed the compressors stopped and the stock is now unsellable. The finance system is encrypted, so you cannot invoice, and the bank cannot see why you have stopped paying the loan covenant. Your biggest customer has a clause allowing termination after seven days of non-supply, and on day five their procurement team starts calling your competitor.

Each of those consequences is survivable on its own. Together, arriving in the same week, they are how companies close. And the control that would have made the difference was not a DLP product. It was knowing that the cold store depended on the corporate network, that the backups could be reached from the domain, and that the customer contract had a seven-day clause.

Start from the consequence, not the threat

The standard risk assessment template starts from assets and threats. List the servers, list the things that could go wrong with them, score each one. This produces a long register in which everything is medium and the two things that would actually close the business are buried somewhere in the middle, scored the same as a lost laptop.

The better approach, and the one we use in fractional CISO engagements, is to start from the other end. Get the leadership team in a room for half a day and ask them to describe, in plain terms, the events that would end the business. Not the IT events, the business events. We cannot fulfil orders for three weeks. We cannot invoice for a month. We lose the regulated status that lets us trade. The stock in the warehouse is destroyed. The product we ship is found to be unsafe. Our largest customer leaves. Most firms land on between three and six of these, and they can usually say roughly how long they could survive each one before cash or contracts ran out.

Only then do you work backwards to the technology. For each business-ending event, what are the systems, suppliers, people and physical dependencies that could cause it? That is where the cold store controller, the single warehouse system, the backups on the same domain and the one person who knows the ERP all surface. They surface because you traced them from a consequence the board already cares about, rather than from a scan result nobody outside IT can interpret.

What a useful assessment looks like

A risk assessment built this way has a few properties that the template version does not. It is short, because it is organised around a handful of scenarios rather than hundreds of assets. It includes operational technology, because the fridges, the conveyors, the door controllers and the building management system turn out to sit on the path to at least one fatal outcome in most physical businesses, and they are almost always missing from an IT-led register. It includes suppliers and contracts, because a termination clause or a single-source logistics provider is as much a dependency as a server. And it produces a tolerable downtime figure for each scenario, agreed by the people who run the business rather than guessed by IT, which is the number every recovery decision should be measured against.

It also changes the conversation about spend. When the board can see that the warehouse system has no tested recovery and the cold store is on the flat network, the DLP evaluation stops looking urgent. Segmenting the operational network, moving backups to an immutable store that domain admin cannot touch, testing a full restore of the warehouse system and reading the customer contracts are not glamorous. They are cheaper than most product purchases. And they are the difference between an incident and an obituary.

Questions to ask this month

If you want to test whether your current assessment is doing its job, these are the questions we put to new clients in the first week.

  • Name the three events that would close the business. Does the risk register mention them in those terms?
  • For each, how long could you survive it? Who agreed that number?
  • Which systems, suppliers, contracts and physical plant sit on the path to each one? When were they last reviewed together rather than separately?
  • Can someone with domain admin reach the backups, the building management system or the warehouse controllers?
  • When did you last restore the system you most depend on, from scratch, and time it?
  • Does your cyber insurance cover business interruption and stock loss from a cyber event, or just the forensics and the lawyers?

If the answers are uncomfortable, that is the assessment working. Fix those before you go back to the DLP shortlist. The shortlist will still be there.

Security is not a list of products. It is knowing which week would be your last, and making sure it never arrives.
